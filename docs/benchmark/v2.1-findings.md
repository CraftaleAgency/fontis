# Benchmark v2.1 — findings

Run `v2.1-next`, 17 November 2026: the `next-steps` branch with seven acts (AI Act, GDPR, Data Act,
NIS2, DORA, Cyber Resilience Act, Digital Services Act), dates of application in the prompt and the
sentence check on. Dataset v2.1: v2 with the DSA-fine and CRA-reporting questions now answerable
(130 answerable, 20 to refuse). `qwen3:14b` answering, `gemma4:12b` judging, `bge-m3` embeddings, one
RTX 3060. Full report: [v2.1-next.md](v2.1-next.md).

The two new questions are answered correctly: the DSA fine (6 % of worldwide annual turnover,

not make retrieval worse: the differences with v2 are within the run-to-run variation seen before
(10 to 13 verdicts out of 120).

## The sentence check

`eval support` asked the judge, for each of the 451 sentences of the 130 answers, whether the full
text of the cited units supports it. Fontis' own check ran on the same answers.

**The figure check works.** It flagged 3 sentences. Two were real errors the other metrics missed: an
Italian answer on NIS2 incident reporting gave the right deadlines (24 and 72 hours) but cited
recital 102 instead of Article 23, which states them. The third was a false positive — "les articles
111 et 113" read as the figure 113 — now fixed: numbers in lists of references are not figures.

**Embedding similarity does not measure support.** Sentences the judge found supported had a median
similarity of 0.787 to their best cited passage; sentences it did not, 0.771. No threshold separates
them better than calling every sentence supported (89% agreement):

| Threshold | Sentences flagged | of which not supported per judge | Supported sentences flagged |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.50 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| 0.55 | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| 0.62 (first guess) | 21 | 6 | 15 |
| 0.70 | 90 | 12 | 78 |

A sentence and the passage it misstates share their words. Similarity remains the right tool to
**locate** the passage and its paragraph and point — the pinpoints checked by hand were right after
the figure-aware choice (GDPR Article 83(5), CRA Article 14(2)(a)) — but not to **judge** it. The
thresholds are now 0.55 / 0.45, so only sentences far from every cited passage are flagged, and the
web app should present the pinpoint and passage as "where to check", not as a verdict.

## NLI experiment

[`bench/experiments/nli_support.py`](../../bench/experiments/nli_support.py) scores each sentence with an
entailment (NLI) model against the paragraphs of the units it cites, in the answer language, and keeps
the highest entailment probability. The 432 sentences with a citation and a judge verdict are the
reference (385 supported, 47 partly or not). AUC: probability that a supported sentence scores above
an unsupported one (0.5 = chance).

| Method | AUC | Unsupported caught (of 47) | Supported flagged |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `bge-m3` similarity (current check), threshold 0.55 | 0.55 | 3 | 2 |
| NLI `multilingual-MiniLMv2-L6-mnli-xnli`, every paragraph, entailment < 0.1 | 0.61 | 11 | 57 |
| same, 4 premises preselected with `bge-m3` (lines, lead-in + line, pairs of lines) | 0.61 | 12 | 42 |

`mDeBERTa-v3-base-xnli` could not be measured: on this machine's CPU it took about 14 s per pair.
It needs the GPU.

**Small NLI models are too literal for this.** Most false alarms are fair paraphrases that a strict
entailment model rejects: "the company must comply with the registration obligations" is not entailed
by "providers of high-risk AI systems shall comply with the registration obligations", because the
answer says "company" where the law says "provider". NLI catches more than similarity, but with
roughly four false alarms per real problem it cannot be shown to users.

## What to do next

- **Support**: try `mDeBERTa` on the GPU machine; failing that, one extra call to the answer model per
  answer, which knows the question ("the company" = the provider asking). Measure either against the
  judge verdicts collected here (`data/results/v2.1-next/support.jsonl`).
- **Figure check across sources**: when a figure is missing from the cited source but present in
  another source of the same answer, suggest that source — the NIS2 case above.
- **The judge itself**: 89% of sentences supported is a judge verdict, and the judge was wrong in
  about one verdict in seven on correctness in v2. Re-read a sample by hand.
- The other optional features (definitions, references, partial refusals) are still off and not
  measured.
